# Kyvernetria (a.k.a. Femenetes): research basis

Kyvernetria is a joke with a serious constraint: every behaviour it borrows from
"average sex differences" has to rest on published evidence, and it has to be
described accurately. This document lists each claim we use, whether the
literature supports it, the corrected wording and numbers, and where they come
from.

Last verified: 2026-09-23. All numbers below were checked against the cited
primary source (abstract or full text) or, where marked, against a reliable
secondary summary of it. Nothing here is taken from memory.

## How to read this document

### Averages, not people

Every difference below is a difference between **group averages**. It tells
you nothing about any individual woman or man. For almost every psychological
trait, the male and female distributions overlap heavily.

Hyde's *gender similarities hypothesis* (Hyde, 2005) reviewed 46
meta-analyses and found that 78% of the reported gender differences were small
(d ≈ 0.11–0.35) or close to zero (d ≤ 0.10). Large differences exist, but they
are the exception.

What Cohen's d means in practice (assuming two normal distributions with equal
variance):

| d | Label (Cohen) | Overlap of the two distributions | Chance that a random woman scores higher than a random man (if women score higher on average) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | small | ~92% | ~56% |
| 0.5 | medium | ~80% | ~64% |
| 0.8 | large | ~69% | ~71% |

(Overlap = 2Φ(−d/2); probability of superiority = Φ(d/√2).)

So even a "large" effect such as the people-vs-things interest difference
(d ≈ 0.93, still ~64% overlap) leaves many women more interested in things
than the average man, and the other way round.

### Causes are mixed

Many of these differences have social and cultural causes as well as
biological ones: noise exposure and smoking in hearing loss, diagnostic bias
in autism, schooling and practice in reading and spatial skills, and alcohol
and occupational risk in the Russian life-expectancy gap. Where a difference
changes across countries or decades, that is strong evidence that the
environment matters. Our choice to model a trait does not mean we think it is
innate or fixed.

### Verdict scale

- **SUPPORTED**: the claim holds as stated.
- **SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION**: a real effect exists, but our wording or
  numbers were wrong. Use the corrected version.
- **WEAK-CONTESTED**: the evidence is small, inconsistent or actively disputed.
- **NOT SUPPORTED**: the evidence contradicts the claim.
- **UNVERIFIED**: we could not check it against a source.

## Biology

### 1. X-inactivation and escape

- **Claim:** X-inactivation is random and clonal (Lyon 1961), so women are
  cellular mosaics. About 15–25% of X-linked genes escape inactivation.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION
- **Corrected wording:** In female mammals, one X chromosome is inactivated in
  each cell early in development. The choice of which X is effectively random
  and is inherited by all descendants of that cell, so female tissues are
  mosaics of two cell populations (Lyon, 1961). Inactivation is incomplete:
  about 15% of X-linked genes escape it to some degree and another ~10% escape
  variably between individuals (Carrel & Willard, 2005, fibroblast assays). A
  tissue-wide analysis found incomplete inactivation in at least 23% of
  X-chromosomal genes (Tukiainen et al., 2017). Correct range: "roughly 15–25%,
  depending on method, tissue and whether variable escapees are counted".
  Mosaicism is often skewed rather than a 50:50 split.
- **Sources:**
  - Lyon, M. F. (1961). Gene action in the X-chromosome of the mouse (*Mus musculus* L.). *Nature*, 190, 372–373. https://doi.org/10.1038/190372a0
  - Carrel, L., & Willard, H. F. (2005). X-inactivation profile reveals extensive variability in X-linked gene expression in females. *Nature*, 434, 400–404. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03479
  - Tukiainen, T., et al. (2017). Landscape of X chromosome inactivation across human tissues. *Nature*, 550, 244–248. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24265

### 2. Red-green colour blindness

- **Claim:** About 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry
  are red-green colour blind.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In populations of Northern European ancestry, red-green colour
  vision deficiency affects about 8% of males and 0.5% of females. The genes
  are X-linked recessive, so a woman needs two affected copies. Prevalence is
  lower in many other populations.
- **Sources:**
  - MedlinePlus Genetics (U.S. National Library of Medicine). Color vision deficiency. https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/color-vision-deficiency/
  - Birch, J. (2012). Worldwide prevalence of red-green color deficiency. *Journal of the Optical Society of America A*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22472762/

### 3. Tetrachromacy in carriers

- **Claim:** Some female carriers may be tetrachromats (Jordan et al., 2010).
- **Verdict:** WEAK-CONTESTED. Plausible, but rare and hard to show.
- **Corrected wording:** Because of X-inactivation, carriers of anomalous
  trichromacy can have four cone types. Having four cone types, however, rarely
  gives four-dimensional colour vision. In the forced-choice test of Jordan et
  al. (2010), only 1 of 25 tested carriers (participant "cDa29"; 1 of 18
  deuteranomaly carriers and 0 of 7 protanomaly carriers) behaved as a strong
  tetrachromat. The authors' later review stresses that rigorous evidence is
  sparse. Say "a very small number of carriers have shown evidence of
  tetrachromacy", not "some women are tetrachromats".
- **Sources:**
  - Jordan, G., Deeb, S. S., Bosten, J. M., & Mollon, J. D. (2010). The dimensionality of color vision in carriers of anomalous trichromacy. *Journal of Vision*, 10(8), 12. https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2191517
  - Jordan, G., & Mollon, J. (2019). Tetrachromacy: the mysterious case of extra-ordinary color vision. *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*. https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/b16c1759-7dbc-4a82-8936-1a64cbbb399a/content

### 4. Immune response and autoimmunity

- **Claim:** Women have stronger innate and adaptive immune responses and
  respond better to vaccines. About 80% of autoimmune patients are women.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (the autoimmunity figure)
- **Corrected wording:** On average, females mount stronger innate and
  adaptive immune responses, including higher antibody responses to many
  vaccines. The flip side is greater susceptibility to autoimmune disease
  (Klein & Flanagan, 2016). The often-quoted "~80% of autoimmune patients are
  women" is an old aggregate figure. The best recent population estimate, from
  22 million UK patient records covering 19 common autoimmune diseases, found
  that 13.1% of women and 7.4% of men were affected (Conrad et al., 2023). That
  is roughly 1.8 times the prevalence in women, and women make up about
  two-thirds of affected people. The female share is much higher for some
  individual diseases. Use: "autoimmune disease is about twice as common in
  women, and for some diseases far more".
- **Sources:**
  - Klein, S. L., & Flanagan, K. L. (2016). Sex differences in immune responses. *Nature Reviews Immunology*, 16, 626–638. https://doi.org/10.1038/nri.2016.90
  - Conrad, N., et al. (2023). Incidence, prevalence, and co-occurrence of autoimmune disorders over time and by age, sex, and socioeconomic status: a population-based cohort study of 22 million individuals in the UK. *The Lancet*. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00457-9 (the 13.1% / 7.4% figures appear in the University of Glasgow release: https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/archiveofnews/2023/may/headline_952084_en.html)

### 5. Life expectancy

- **Claim:** Women live about 5 years longer worldwide (WHO). In Russia the gap
  is about 10 years.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In WHO Global Health Estimates, global life expectancy at birth
  was 75.7 years for females and 70.6 for males in 2019 (a gap of 5.1 years),
  and 74.0 vs 68.9 in 2021 (also 5.1 years). Russia has one of the largest
  gaps in the world: 9.8 years in the WHO estimates for 2019, and 78.7 vs 68.0
  years (10.7 years) in Rosstat data for 2023. Much of the Russian gap reflects
  male alcohol use, smoking, injuries and cardiovascular mortality, not biology
  alone.
- **Sources:**
  - WHO Global Health Observatory, indicator WHOSIS_000001 (life expectancy at birth), Global Health Estimates 2021. https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/life-expectancy-at-birth-(years) (values retrieved via the GHO OData API)
  - Rosstat data as reported by Demoscope Weekly (HSE Institute of Demography), 2025. https://www.demoscope.ru/weekly/2025/01069/barom04.php

### 6. Body composition and strength

- **Claim:** Women have a higher body-fat percentage. Upper-body strength is
  about 50–60% of the male average, lower-body about 65–70%.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In U.S. NHANES DXA data, fat makes up about 38% of body weight
  in women and about 24% in men at age 25, a gap of about 11–12 percentage
  points that stays fairly constant with age (Kelly et al., 2009). In a
  classic small study (8 women, 8 men), women were about 52% as strong as men
  in the upper body and 66% in the lower body, mainly because men had larger
  muscle fibres and muscle cross-sectional area (Miller et al., 1993). Keep the
  wording "roughly half (upper body) and two-thirds (lower body)" and note the
  small sample.
- **Sources:**
  - Kelly, T. L., Wilson, K. E., & Heymsfield, S. B. (2009). Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry body composition reference values from NHANES. *PLoS ONE*, 4(9), e7038. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0007038
  - Miller, A. E., MacDougall, J. D., Tarnopolsky, M. A., & Sale, D. G. (1993). Gender differences in strength and muscle fiber characteristics. *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, 66, 254–262. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00235103

### 7. Fatigue resistance and "ultra-endurance advantage"

- **Claim:** Women resist fatigue better in submaximal isometric tasks, which
  gives them an ultra-endurance advantage.
- **Verdict:** First half SUPPORTED. The ultra-endurance advantage is NOT
  SUPPORTED as a performance claim.
- **Corrected wording:** For isometric contractions at the same relative
  intensity, women are usually less fatigable than men. The effect depends on
  the task: it shrinks or disappears for some dynamic and high-intensity tasks
  (Hunter, 2014, 2016). Some female traits, such as greater fatigue
  resistance and fat oxidation, would be expected to help in very long events,
  but others hurt performance, such as lower oxygen-carrying capacity (Tiller
  et al., 2021). Women do **not** outperform men in ultra-endurance
  competition. The gap narrows with distance in some analyses, but a sizeable
  gap favouring men remains among top finishers (Sitko et al., 2025). Use
  "less fatigable at sustained submaximal effort", never "better at
  ultra-endurance".
- **Sources:**
  - Hunter, S. K. (2014). Sex differences in human fatigability: mechanisms and insight to physiological responses. *Acta Physiologica*, 210(4), 768–789. https://doi.org/10.1111/apha.12234
  - Hunter, S. K. (2016). The relevance of sex differences in performance fatigability. *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 48(11), 2247–2256. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000928
  - Tiller, N. B., et al. (2021). Do sex differences in physiology confer a female advantage in ultra-endurance sport? *Sports Medicine*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01417-2
  - Sitko, S., López-Laval, I., Cirer-Sastre, R., & Valenzuela, P. L. (2025). Ultra-endurance performance: Are women closing the gap? A systematic review and meta-analysis. *International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching*. https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541251354909

### 8. Drug dosing and adverse drug reactions

- **Claim:** In 2013 the FDA halved the recommended zolpidem dose for women.
  Women also differ in pharmacokinetics and have more adverse drug reactions.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In January 2013 the FDA lowered the recommended starting dose of
  zolpidem for women from 10 mg to 5 mg for immediate-release products, and
  from 12.5 mg to 6.25 mg for extended-release products. The reason was
  next-morning impairment: women clear the drug more slowly. The FDA
  suggested that prescribers consider the lower dose for men too. Across 86
  drugs with established sex differences in pharmacokinetics, women given the
  same dose usually had higher blood concentrations and slower elimination.
  For 96% of drugs with higher values in women, women also had more adverse
  drug reactions (Zucker & Prendergast, 2020).
- **Sources:**
  - U.S. FDA (2013). Drug Safety Communication: Risk of next-morning impairment after use of insomnia drugs; FDA requires lower recommended doses for certain drugs containing zolpidem. https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/questions-and-answers-risk-next-morning-impairment-after-use-insomnia-drugs-fda-requires-lower
  - Zucker, I., & Prendergast, B. J. (2020). Sex differences in pharmacokinetics predict adverse drug reactions in women. *Biology of Sex Differences*, 11. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13293-020-00308-5

### 9. Heart attack symptoms

- **Claim:** Heart attacks in women more often present without classic chest
  pain, with "atypical" symptoms.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION
- **Corrected wording:** Chest pain or discomfort is the most common symptom
  of heart attack in **both** sexes. Women more often also report other
  symptoms, such as shortness of breath, fatigue, weakness, nausea or
  indigestion, and these can lead to delays in diagnosis (Mehta et al., 2016).
  The 2021 AHA/ACC chest-pain guideline advises against calling these symptoms
  "atypical". Do not say "women don't get chest pain"; say "women more often
  have accompanying non-chest-pain symptoms".
- **Sources:**
  - Mehta, L. S., et al. (2016). Acute myocardial infarction in women: a scientific statement from the American Heart Association. *Circulation*, 133(9), 916–947. https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000000351
  - Gulati, M., et al. (2021). 2021 AHA/ACC/ASE/CHEST/SAEM/SCCT/SCMR guideline for the evaluation and diagnosis of chest pain: executive summary. *Circulation*. https://doi.org/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001030

### 10. Sense of smell

- **Claim:** Women have a slightly better sense of smell on average; women's
  olfactory bulbs have more cells.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION. The effect is tiny.
- **Corrected wording:** A meta-analysis of more than 8,000 people per test
  found that women outperform men on every olfactory measure, but the effects
  are small to trivial (g = 0.08–0.30; identification g ≈ 0.08). The authors
  themselves call them trivial (Sorokowski et al., 2019). A post-mortem study
  found about 43% more cells in female olfactory bulbs, but it examined only
  7 men and 11 women aged 55–94 (Oliveira-Pinto et al., 2014), so treat it as
  preliminary.
- **Sources:**
  - Sorokowski, P., et al. (2019). Sex differences in human olfaction: a meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 242. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00242
  - Oliveira-Pinto, A. V., et al. (2014). Sexual dimorphism in the human olfactory bulb: females have more neurons and glial cells than males. *PLoS ONE*, 9(11), e111733. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0111733

### 11. High-frequency hearing with age

- **Claim:** Women keep their high-frequency hearing better with age.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION
- **Corrected wording:** In longitudinal data from the Baltimore Longitudinal
  Study of Aging (681 men and 416 women screened for ear disease and noise
  exposure), hearing thresholds declined faster in men, especially at high
  frequencies, and male decline was detectable from about age 30 (Pearson et
  al., 1995). Part of the male disadvantage in the general population comes
  from occupational noise and smoking. At very old ages, women can have worse
  low-frequency thresholds.
- **Sources:**
  - Pearson, J. D., Morrell, C. H., Gordon-Salant, S., et al. (1995). Gender differences in a longitudinal study of age-associated hearing loss. *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 97, 1196–1205. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/15323670_Gender_differences_in_a_longitudinal_study_of_age-associated_hearing_loss

### 12. Brain size, "mosaic" brains and classifiers

- **Claim:** Men have larger total brain volume. Individual brains are
  mosaics of male- and female-typical features (Joel et al., 2015), yet
  multivariate classifiers predict sex with over 90% accuracy.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (both findings hold and do not contradict each other)
- **Wording:** On average, males have larger brains: about 10.8% greater total
  brain volume, 12% greater intracranial volume and 9.4% more grey matter
  (Ruigrok et al., 2014). Joel et al. (2015) showed that few individual brains
  are consistently "male-typical" or "female-typical" across regions, which
  they described as a mosaic. Multivariate classifiers using the same kind of
  data still predict sex with about 93% accuracy (Chekroud et al., 2016;
  Anderson et al., 2019, forensic sample of about 1,300 people). Much of this
  signal is tied to head and brain size. Neither result means there are two
  brain "types" that predict behaviour.
- **Sources:**
  - Ruigrok, A. N. V., et al. (2014). A meta-analysis of sex differences in human brain structure. *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 39, 34–50. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2013.12.004
  - Joel, D., et al. (2015). Sex beyond the genitalia: the human brain mosaic. *PNAS*, 112(50), 15468–15473. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1509654112
  - Chekroud, A. M., Ward, E. J., Rosenberg, M. D., & Holmes, A. J. (2016). Patterns in the human brain mosaic discriminate males from females. *PNAS*, 113(14), E1968. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1523888113
  - Anderson, N. E., et al. (2019). Machine learning of brain gray matter differentiates sex in a large forensic sample. *Human Brain Mapping*. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6397072/

## Cognition

### 13. General intelligence and male variability

- **Claim:** There is no mean sex difference in general intelligence. The
  greater male variability hypothesis is contested.
- **Verdict:** The no-mean-difference part is SUPPORTED. Greater male
  variability is WEAK-CONTESTED.
- **Wording:** Reviews find no meaningful average sex difference in general
  intelligence (Halpern et al., 2007). Many datasets show slightly greater
  male variance: for example, a variance ratio of 1.08 in U.S. mathematics
  data (Lindberg et al., 2010) and greater male variance in g in Scottish
  cohort data (Johnson, Carothers & Deary, 2008). The size of the ratio varies
  across countries, traits and cohorts, and its causes are disputed. Do not
  model it.
- **Sources:**
  - Halpern, D. F., et al. (2007). The science of sex differences in science and mathematics. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 8(1), 1–51. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1529-1006.2007.00032.x
  - Johnson, W., Carothers, A., & Deary, I. J. (2008). Sex differences in variability in general intelligence: a new look at the old question. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00096.x
  - Lindberg, S. M., Hyde, J. S., Petersen, J. L., & Linn, M. C. (2010). New trends in gender and mathematics performance: a meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 136(6), 1123–1135. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21038941/

### 14. Verbal ability, fluency and reading

- **Claim:** Women have an advantage in verbal fluency; the overall verbal
  difference is small (Hyde & Linn, 1988); girls read better in PISA.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (fluency applies to letter-based
  tasks only)
- **Corrected wording:** The overall verbal-ability difference is negligible,
  d ≈ 0.1 (Hyde & Linn, 1988). In verbal fluency, women have a small advantage
  on **phonemic** (letter-based) fluency (d = 0.12–0.13) but show essentially
  no difference on **semantic** (category) fluency (d = 0.01–0.02)
  (Hirnstein et al., 2023). The reading gap is robust: in PISA 2022, girls
  outscored boys in reading by 24 points on average across OECD countries, and
  scored higher in 79 of 81 participating systems.
- **Sources:**
  - Hyde, J. S., & Linn, M. C. (1988). Gender differences in verbal ability: a meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 104(1), 53–69. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.104.1.53
  - Hirnstein, M., Stuebs, J., Moè, A., & Hausmann, M. (2023). Sex/gender differences in verbal fluency and verbal-episodic memory: a meta-analysis. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 18(1), 67–90. https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916221082116
  - OECD (2023). *PISA 2022 Results (Volume I)*. https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en.html

### 15. Episodic memory

- **Claim:** Women have better episodic memory (Asperholm et al., 2019).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (small)
- **Wording:** A meta-analysis of 617 studies with about 1.2 million
  participants found a small female advantage, g = 0.19. It was larger for
  verbal material (g = 0.28), and men had an advantage on tasks that rely on
  spatial processing. Hirnstein et al. (2023) found d = 0.28 for verbal recall.
- **Sources:**
  - Asperholm, M., Högman, N., Rafi, J., & Herlitz, A. (2019). What did you do yesterday? A meta-analysis of sex differences in episodic memory. *Psychological Bulletin*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31180695/
  - Hirnstein et al. (2023), see claim 14.

### 16. Object-location memory

- **Claim:** Women have better object-location memory (Voyer et al., 2007).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (depends on the task)
- **Corrected wording:** The meta-analysis (36 studies, 123 effect sizes)
  found female advantages for object-identity memory and, above age 13, for
  most object-location tasks. There were exceptions: no reliable female
  advantage for feminine, uncommon or gender-neutral objects, and a **male**
  advantage for masculine objects and for distance-based scoring. Describe it
  as "a small-to-moderate, task-dependent female advantage in remembering
  where things were". We found no single overall d in the abstract.
- **Sources:**
  - Voyer, D., Postma, A., Brake, B., & Imperato-McGinley, J. (2007). Gender differences in object location memory: a meta-analysis. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 14(1), 23–38. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194024

### 17. Emotion recognition

- **Claim:** Women are better at recognising emotions (Thompson & Voyer, 2014).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (small)
- **Wording:** A multilevel meta-analysis of 551 effect sizes found a small
  overall female advantage in recognising non-verbal emotion displays,
  d = 0.19. The size depended on the emotion, the sensory modality, the
  actor's sex and the participant's age.
- **Sources:**
  - Thompson, A. E., & Voyer, D. (2014). Sex differences in the ability to recognise non-verbal displays of emotion: a meta-analysis. *Cognition and Emotion*, 28(7). https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2013.875889

### 18. Processing speed

- **Claim:** Women have a small processing-speed advantage (Roivainen, 2011).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (depends on the task)
- **Corrected wording:** Women are faster on processing-speed tasks involving
  digits, letters and rapid naming. Men are faster on simple reaction time and
  finger tapping. The female advantage may be partly explained by reading and
  writing fluency. Do not describe this as general "faster processing".
- **Sources:**
  - Roivainen, E. (2011). Gender differences in processing speed: a review of recent research. *Learning and Individual Differences*, 21(2), 145–149. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lindif.2010.11.021

### 19. Mental rotation

- **Claim:** Men have an advantage in mental rotation, d ≈ 0.5–0.9.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** Men have a moderate advantage. Voyer et al. (1995) found
  d ≈ 0.56 across mental-rotation tests and about 0.9 on the Vandenberg–Kuse
  test (the 0.94 figure comes from a secondary summary). Other estimates are
  g = 0.57 on the PSVT:R, where time limits increase the gap (Maeda & Yoon,
  2013), and d = 0.60 in studies published since 2010 (Hervin & Gibson, 2026).
  The gap responds to training and experience.
- **Sources:**
  - Voyer, D., Voyer, S., & Bryden, M. P. (1995). Magnitude of sex differences in spatial abilities: a meta-analysis and consideration of critical variables. *Psychological Bulletin*, 117(2). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.117.2.250
  - Maeda, Y., & Yoon, S. Y. (2013). A meta-analysis on gender differences in mental rotation ability measured by the Purdue Spatial Visualization Tests: Visualization of Rotations (PSVT:R). *Educational Psychology Review*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-012-9215-x
  - Hervin, J., & Gibson, C. L. (2026). A systematic review and meta-analysis examining sex differences in mental rotation ability. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218261419079

### 20. Mathematics

- **Claim:** There is almost no average difference in mathematics (Lindberg
  et al., 2010).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** Across 242 studies (1990–2007) with 1.29 million participants,
  d = 0.05 and the variance ratio was 1.08, meaning no meaningful average
  difference and nearly equal variances.
- **Sources:** Lindberg et al. (2010), see claim 13.

## Personality and behaviour

### 21. People-vs-things interests

- **Claim:** Women are more interested in people and men in things,
  d ≈ 0.9–0.93 (Su, Rounds & Armstrong, 2009).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In technical manuals for 47 interest inventories covering
  503,188 respondents, d = 0.93 on the Things–People dimension. This is one of
  the largest psychological sex differences, but the distributions still
  overlap by about 64%. It measures **interest**, not ability.
- **Sources:**
  - Su, R., Rounds, J., & Armstrong, P. I. (2009). Men and things, women and people: a meta-analysis of sex differences in interests. *Psychological Bulletin*, 135(6), 859–884. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19883140/

### 22. Agreeableness and neuroticism

- **Claim:** Women score higher on agreeableness and neuroticism.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (small to moderate)
- **Wording:** In self-report data, women score higher on Agreeableness
  (d ≈ 0.48; Compassion aspect 0.45, Politeness 0.36) and Neuroticism
  (d ≈ 0.39; Withdrawal 0.40, Volatility 0.30). Differences on Extraversion,
  Conscientiousness and Openness are near zero at the trait level but diverge
  at the aspect level (Weisberg et al., 2011). Across 26 cultures the
  differences were "small relative to individual variation within genders"
  (Costa et al., 2001). These are self-reports and may partly reflect
  reporting norms.
- **Sources:**
  - Costa, P. T., Terracciano, A., & McCrae, R. R. (2001). Gender differences in personality traits across cultures: robust and surprising findings. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81(2), 322–331. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.2.322
  - Weisberg, Y. J., DeYoung, C. G., & Hirsh, J. B. (2011). Gender differences in personality across the ten aspects of the Big Five. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2, 178. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00178

### 23. Gender-equality paradox

- **Claim:** Personality differences are larger in more gender-equal
  countries (Schmitt et al., 2008; Falk & Hermle, 2018).
- **Verdict:** WEAK-CONTESTED
- **Wording:** Schmitt et al. (2008; 55 nations) and Falk and Hermle (2018;
  76 countries, about 80,000 people, economic preferences) reported larger sex
  differences in richer, more gender-equal countries. Reanalyses argue that
  the pattern is fragile. Ilmarinen and Lönnqvist (2024) show that
  country-level difference scores are hard to interpret. Other critics point to
  spatial non-independence between neighbouring countries, ad hoc equality
  indices and measurement non-invariance across cultures. Mention it only as a
  debated finding, and do not build features on it.
- **Sources:**
  - Schmitt, D. P., Realo, A., Voracek, M., & Allik, J. (2008). Why can't a man be more like a woman? Sex differences in Big Five personality traits across 55 cultures. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 94(1), 168–182. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.1.168
  - Falk, A., & Hermle, J. (2018). Relationship of gender differences in preferences to economic development and gender equality. *Science*, 362(6412), eaas9899. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aas9899
  - Ilmarinen, V.-J., & Lönnqvist, J.-E. (2024). Deconstructing the gender-equality paradox. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 127(1), 217–237. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38900533/

### 24. Physical and indirect aggression

- **Claim:** Women are less physically aggressive (Archer, 2004). Indirect or
  relational aggression is at similar levels (Card et al., 2008).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (age and measurement matter)
- **Corrected wording:** Direct, and especially physical, aggression is more
  common in males at all ages sampled and across cultures, peaking between
  ages 20 and 30. Effect sizes are moderate to large, roughly d ≈ 0.3–0.9
  depending on the measure; this range comes from secondary summaries of
  Archer (2004). For indirect aggression in childhood and adolescence, sex
  differences are trivial (Card et al., 2008). With peer reports, Archer found
  a slight and growing female lead in indirect aggression with age. Say
  "similar or slightly higher in girls", not "equal".
- **Sources:**
  - Archer, J. (2004). Sex differences in aggression in real-world settings: a meta-analytic review. *Review of General Psychology*, 8(4), 291–322. https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.8.4.291
  - Card, N. A., Stucky, B. D., Sawalani, G. M., & Little, T. D. (2008). Direct and indirect aggression during childhood and adolescence: a meta-analytic review of gender differences, intercorrelations, and relations to maladjustment. *Child Development*, 79, 1185–1229. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2008.01184.x

### 25. Risk taking

- **Claim:** Women take fewer risks (Byrnes et al., 1999).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (small, depends on the domain)
- **Wording:** Across 150 studies, males took more risks in 14 of 16 risk
  categories, and nearly half of the average effects exceeded d = 0.20. The
  size varied by domain: it was larger for intellectual and physical-skill
  risks and smaller for others, such as smoking. We could not verify an
  overall mean d from the abstract, so do not quote one.
- **Sources:**
  - Byrnes, J. P., Miller, D. C., & Schafer, W. D. (1999). Gender differences in risk taking: a meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 125(3), 367–383. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.3.367

### 26. Depression and anxiety

- **Claim:** Depression is about 2× and anxiety about 1.5–2× more common in
  women (Salk et al., 2017).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** For major depression the odds ratio is 1.95 (95% CI
  1.88–2.03); for depressive symptoms d = 0.27. The gap appears around age 12
  and peaks in adolescence (OR = 3.02 at ages 13–15), then stays stable in
  adulthood (Salk et al., 2017; data from over 90 countries). For anxiety, the
  female-to-male lifetime prevalence of any anxiety disorder was 1.7:1 in U.S.
  data, with no difference for social anxiety disorder (McLean et al., 2011).
- **Sources:**
  - Salk, R. H., Hyde, J. S., & Abramson, L. Y. (2017). Gender differences in depression in representative national samples: meta-analyses of diagnoses and symptoms. *Psychological Bulletin*, 143(8), 783–822. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000102
  - McLean, C. P., Asnaani, A., Litz, B. T., & Hofmann, S. G. (2011). Gender differences in anxiety disorders: prevalence, course of illness, comorbidity and burden of illness. *Journal of Psychiatric Research*, 45, 1027–1035. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2011.03.006

### 27. Autism ratio and camouflaging

- **Claim:** The male-to-female autism ratio is about 3:1, not 4:1 (Loomes et
  al., 2017), and camouflaging contributes (Hull et al., 2020).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** The pooled male-to-female odds ratio across all studies was
  4.20, but high-quality studies gave 3.32 and population-screening studies
  gave 3.25. The authors conclude that the true ratio is closer to 3:1 and
  that there is a diagnostic bias against girls. Camouflaging (masking
  autistic traits) is one proposed reason autistic girls and women are missed;
  the evidence base is still developing (Hull et al., 2020, narrative review).
- **Sources:**
  - Loomes, R., Hull, L., & Mandy, W. P. L. (2017). What is the male-to-female ratio in autism spectrum disorder? A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry*, 56(6), 466–474. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2017.03.013
  - Hull, L., Petrides, K. V., & Mandy, W. (2020). The female autism phenotype and camouflaging: a narrative review. *Review Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, 7, 306–317. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40489-020-00197-9

## Speech and language

### 28. Words per day

- **Claim:** Mehl et al. (2007) found about 16,215 words a day for women and
  15,669 for men, not a significant difference.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (with an age nuance from a 2025 replication)
- **Wording:** In 396 university students (210 women, 186 men) who wore audio
  recorders, women spoke on average 16,215 words a day and men 15,669
  (d ≈ 0.07). The difference was not significant, and the authors concluded
  that the stereotype of female talkativeness is unfounded. A larger
  registered-report replication (2,197 participants aged 10–94, 22 samples in
  four countries) again found no overall sex difference. It did find a gap in
  ages 25–64: 21,845 words a day for women vs 18,570 for men. There was no
  difference in adolescence, emerging adulthood or older age. Summary: "no
  overall difference; possibly more talk by women in mid-adulthood".
- **Sources:**
  - Mehl, M. R., Vazire, S., Ramírez-Esparza, N., Slatcher, R. B., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2007). Are women really more talkative than men? *Science*, 317(5834), 82. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1139940
  - Tidwell, C., Pfeifer, V. A., Danvers, A., Mehl, M. R., et al. (2025). Are women really (not) more talkative than men? A registered report of binary gender similarities/differences in daily word use. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Summary: https://news.arizona.edu/news/do-women-talk-more-men-it-might-depend-their-age ; record: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/384083312 (the author order is taken from the university release and is not confirmed)

### 29. Early language development

- **Claim:** Girls develop language earlier and have larger early vocabularies
  (Eriksson et al., 2012).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (small)
- **Wording:** Across 13,783 children from 10 European language communities,
  girls were slightly ahead of boys in early gestures, productive vocabulary
  and word combinations, and the gap grew with age up to about 2.5 years. The
  effect of sex was small, and boys were not more variable than girls. We
  could not verify the exact percentage of variance explained.
- **Sources:**
  - Eriksson, M., et al. (2012). Differences between girls and boys in emerging language skills: evidence from 10 language communities. *British Journal of Developmental Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-835X.2011.02042.x

### 30. Stuttering and dyslexia ratios

- **Claim:** Stuttering is about 4:1 male in adults; dyslexia is also more
  common in boys (Rutter et al., 2004).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION
- **Corrected wording:** Near onset, stuttering is only slightly more common
  in boys (about 1.3–2.2:1). The ratio widens with age because girls recover
  more often, reaching the traditionally cited 4:1 or more in adults (Yairi &
  Ambrose, 2013). A community survey across the whole lifespan found 2.3:1
  overall (Craig et al., 2002), so "about 4:1" should be limited to older
  children and adults in clinical samples. For reading disability, four
  epidemiological cohorts found boys more often affected, with odds ratios of
  1.39–3.19; the difference held when IQ was taken into account (Rutter et
  al., 2004). Say "roughly 1.5–3 times as common in boys", not a fixed ratio.
- **Sources:**
  - Yairi, E., & Ambrose, N. (2013). Epidemiology of stuttering: 21st century advances. *Journal of Fluency Disorders*, 38(2), 66–87. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3687212/
  - Craig, A., Hancock, K., Tran, Y., Craig, M., & Peters, K. (2002). Epidemiology of stuttering in the community across the entire life span. *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research*, 45(6), 1097–1105. https://doi.org/10.1044/1092-4388(2002/088)
  - Rutter, M., et al. (2004). Sex differences in developmental reading disability: new findings from 4 epidemiological studies. *JAMA*, 291(16), 2007–2012. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.291.16.2007

### 31. Women and linguistic change

- **Claim:** Women lead most linguistic changes and use fewer stigmatised
  variants of stable variables (Labov, 1990, 2001).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED (as a sociolinguistic generalisation)
- **Wording:** Labov's two principles are: (1) for stable sociolinguistic
  variables, men use stigmatised non-standard forms more often than women; and
  (2) in change from below, women are usually the innovators and lead men.
  Labov also describes a "gender paradox": women conform more to overt
  standard norms but lead changes that are not yet noticed. Men lead some
  changes, and the pattern interacts with social class.
- **Sources:**
  - Labov, W. (1990). The intersection of sex and social class in the course of linguistic change. *Language Variation and Change*, 2(2), 205–254. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954394500000338
  - Labov, W. (2001). *Principles of Linguistic Change, Vol. 2: Social Factors*. Oxford: Blackwell.

### 32. Word use

- **Claim:** Women use more pronouns and social and emotion words; men use
  more articles and numbers. The effects are small (Newman et al., 2008).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** In more than 14,000 text samples from 70 studies, women used
  more words about psychological and social processes (pronouns, verbs,
  social words). Men used more numbers, articles, long words and swear words,
  and referred more to object properties and impersonal topics. Differences
  were small and larger in less constrained tasks. Do not quote a specific d,
  because the abstract gives none.
- **Sources:**
  - Newman, M. L., Groom, C. J., Handelman, L. D., & Pennebaker, J. W. (2008). Gender differences in language use: an analysis of 14,000 text samples. *Discourse Processes*, 45(3), 211–236. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530802073712

### 33. Tannen's "rapport talk vs report talk"

- **Claim:** Women use "rapport talk" and men use "report talk" (Tannen).
- **Verdict:** NOT SUPPORTED as an empirical generalisation
- **Wording:** Tannen's *You Just Don't Understand* (1990) is a popular,
  qualitative book based on selected examples. Systematic reviews of its
  evidence found problems with adequacy, relevance and consistency. Empirical
  searches for systematic male–female "miscommunication" have largely failed,
  and language-and-gender researchers such as Deborah Cameron reject the
  model. Mention it only as a popular idea.
- **Sources:**
  - Tannen, D. (1990). *You Just Don't Understand: Women and Men in Conversation*. New York: William Morrow.
  - Goldsmith, D. J., & Fulfs, P. A. (1999). "You just don't have the evidence": an analysis of claims and evidence in Deborah Tannen's *You Just Don't Understand*. *Annals of the International Communication Association*, 22(1). https://doi.org/10.1080/23808985.1999.11678958
  - Cameron, D. (2007). *The Myth of Mars and Venus*. Oxford University Press.

### 34. Lakoff's hedging claims

- **Claim:** Lakoff (1975) said women hedge more. The claim is only weakly
  supported (Leaper & Robnett, 2011, d ≈ 0.23).
- **Verdict:** WEAK-CONTESTED (small and depends on context)
- **Wording:** A meta-analysis of 29 studies (3,502 participants) found that
  women were slightly more likely to use tentative language, d = 0.23, and
  that the size depended strongly on context. The authors suggest that
  tentative speech often reflects interpersonal sensitivity or lower status
  in the situation, rather than female uncertainty.
- **Sources:**
  - Lakoff, R. (1975). *Language and Woman's Place*. New York: Harper & Row.
  - Leaper, C., & Robnett, R. D. (2011). Women are more likely than men to use tentative language, aren't they? A meta-analysis testing for gender differences and moderators. *Psychology of Women Quarterly*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0361684310392728

### 35. Voice pitch

- **Claim:** Typical adult fundamental frequency is about 200–220 Hz in women
  and 100–120 Hz in men.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED WITH CORRECTION (the female range is too high and too
  narrow)
- **Corrected wording:** Women's speaking F0 is typically about 1.5–1.8 times
  men's. Titze (1989) attributes a factor of about 1.6 to the length of the
  vocal folds. Absolute values depend on age, loudness, language and method.
  In a German population sample of 2,472 adults aged 40–79, conversational F0
  was about 169 Hz for women and 112 Hz for men, and about 198 Hz and 130 Hz
  at classroom loudness. The authors note that female values were lower than
  previously described (Berg et al., 2017). Use "roughly 165–220 Hz for
  women and 100–130 Hz for men".
- **Sources:**
  - Titze, I. R. (1989). Physiologic and acoustic differences between male and female voices. *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, 85(4), 1699–1707. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.397959
  - Berg, M., Fuchs, M., Wirkner, K., Loeffler, M., Engel, C., & Berger, T. (2017). The speaking voice in the general population: normative data and associations to sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. *Journal of Voice*, 31(2), 257.e13–257.e24. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2016.06.001

## Engineering

### 36. N-version programming and correlated failures

- **Claim:** N-version programming (Avizienis, 1985) has limits: failures are
  correlated (Knight & Leveson, 1986).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED
- **Wording:** N-version programming runs several independently developed
  versions and takes a majority vote. Its reliability gain assumes the
  versions fail independently (Avizienis, 1985). Knight and Leveson tested 27
  independently written versions against one million inputs. Each version was
  highly reliable, but coincident failures were far more frequent than
  independence predicts. Diversity helps, but it is not independence. The
  same applies to Kyvernetria's two "allele" builds.
- **Sources:**
  - Avizienis, A. (1985). The N-version approach to fault-tolerant software. *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, SE-11(12), 1491–1501. https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1985.231893
  - Knight, J. C., & Leveson, N. G. (1986). An experimental evaluation of the assumption of independence in multiversion programming. *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, SE-12(1), 96–109. https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1986.6312924

### Intro reference

- Hyde, J. S. (2005). The gender similarities hypothesis. *American Psychologist*, 60(6), 581–592. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.60.6.581

## What we deliberately do NOT model

- **Menstrual-cycle mood swings ("PMS mode", cyclical instability).** Popular
  stereotypes greatly overstate cycle effects on mood and cognition for most
  women, and a "cyclical failure mode" would be both inaccurate and demeaning.
- **"Women talk more" (chattier logs, verbose output).** There is no overall
  difference (Mehl et al., 2007; 2025 replication, claim 28). The only gap
  appears in one age band and does not support a general stereotype.
- **Rapport vs report talk, male–female miscommunication.** Not supported
  empirically (claim 33).
- **Hedging, uncertainty or "less confident" defaults.** The tentative-language
  effect is small and depends on context (d ≈ 0.23, claim 34). It reflects the
  situation, not competence.
- **Lower intelligence, math or logic ability.** There is no average
  difference (claims 13 and 20).
- **Greater male variability (e.g. "fewer extreme outliers").** Contested, and
  it varies across countries and traits (claim 13).
- **Gender-equality paradox.** Methodologically disputed (claim 23).
- **Ultra-endurance superiority.** Not supported by performance data
  (claim 7).
- **Tetrachromacy as a general female trait.** Only one carrier has shown
  strong tetrachromacy in a controlled test (claim 3).
- **"Hysteria", emotional instability or irrationality.** These are
  stereotypes, not findings. The neuroticism difference is moderate
  (d ≈ 0.39) with large overlap and does not imply irrationality.
- **Weaker or "delicate" hardware in general.** Differences in strength are
  real (claim 6), but they say nothing about reliability, and the model uses
  greater fatigue resistance instead (claim 7).
- **Anything about individuals.** Kyvernetria models averages and never
  predicts or labels any specific person.
